# Keyword schema

Each section below is one category of keywords.

## Cuisine

Assign the broad cuisine the dish belongs to: the country or culture a home cook
would name if asked what kind of food this is. Examples: Chinese, Japanese,
Thai, Indian, Mexican, Italian, French, American, Korean, Vietnamese.

Then ask where the dish comes from. Most named dishes trace to a specific
region, and whenever you can name that region confidently, assign it as a second
cuisine keyword alongside the broad one:

- Tandoori chicken -> Indian + Punjabi
- Mapo tofu -> Chinese + Szechuan
- Gumbo -> American + Cajun
- Carbonara -> Italian + Roman
- Cochinita pibil -> Mexican + Yucatecan
- Bibimbap -> Korean + Jeonju

A region keyword never appears on its own: whenever you assign one, assign its
broad cuisine alongside it. Cajun always comes with American, Szechuan always
comes with Chinese.

The region must name a place - a region, province, state, or city - not a kind
of dish. Sushi, curry, barbecue, and ramen are dish categories, not regions, and
never belong here.

Skip the region only when you genuinely do not know where the dish is from, or
when it has no particular origin - a plain roast chicken is French, full stop.
Do not skip it because the recipe is a modern, simplified, or weeknight version
of the dish; a shortcut carbonara is still Roman.

When a dish deliberately combines two cuisines - Korean tacos, kimchi
quesadillas, miso carbonara - assign the broad cuisine for each of them, plus
the keyword Fusion.

Only call something Fusion when both traditions are doing real work in the dish.
A single borrowed ingredient is not fusion: soy sauce in a steak marinade does
not make it Japanese, and sriracha on scrambled eggs does not make them Thai.

A dish that carries a name from a particular food culture always has a cuisine,
however simple its ingredients: focaccia is Italian, hummus is Levantine, soda
bread is Irish, congee is Chinese. Simplicity is never a reason to skip the
cuisine.

Leave cuisine off only for food that belongs to no tradition at all - a green
smoothie, a basic vinaigrette, roasted vegetables, a plain grilled chicken
breast.

## Dish type

Assign exactly one of these:

- Main - the centerpiece of a meal
- Side - served alongside a main
- Bread - a bread, roll, flatbread, or other yeasted or quick bread
- Appetizer - served before the meal, or as a small bite
- Breakfast - eaten as a morning meal
- Dessert - sweet, served after the meal
- Snack - eaten on its own, outside of a meal
- Cocktail - an alcoholic mixed drink
- Coffee - a coffee drink or preparation
- Drink - any other beverage
- Sauce - a sauce, dressing, marinade, or condiment made to use in or on
  something else
- Basic - a staple or building block made to be used in other recipes (stock,
  pasta dough, spice blend, pickles)

Bread wins over Side and Snack - a focaccia is Bread even when it is served
alongside a main. Sweet baked goods eaten as dessert (cake, cookies, pie) are
Dessert, not Bread.

Cocktail and Coffee always win over Drink - use Drink only for a beverage that
is neither.

If a dish plausibly fits two, pick the one matching how it is most often eaten.

## Diet

The only two keywords in this category are Vegetarian and Vegan. Never invent
another one, and never assign a keyword describing what a dish contains or what
it is not. A recipe gets both of these keywords, one, or neither.

Before assigning Vegetarian, read the ingredient list and try to name a meat,
poultry, or seafood in it. If you can name one - chicken, beef, pork, fish,
shrimp, bacon, anything - then neither keyword applies and you are done with
this category. Say which ingredient it was in your reason.

Assign Vegetarian only when that search comes up empty.

Additionally assign Vegan when it also contains no dairy, eggs, or honey. A
vegan dish always gets both keywords, so that filtering on Vegetarian finds
every meat-free recipe.

Most recipes get neither. That is expected - do not stretch to assign one, and
do not add anything to say so.

Check the ingredients carefully before assigning either one, because the
disqualifying ingredient is usually a background one. Meat stock, fish sauce,
oyster sauce, shrimp paste, anchovies, Worcestershire sauce, lard, and gelatin
all rule out Vegetarian. Butter, ghee, cream, milk, yogurt, cheese, and honey
all rule out Vegan. These are things to check for, not keywords to assign.

If a disqualifying ingredient is listed at all, leave the keyword off - even
when the recipe calls it optional, and even when it is only a garnish or a
serving suggestion. Leaving the keyword off a dish that qualifies is a small
problem; putting it on a dish that doesn't is a real one.
